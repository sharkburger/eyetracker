01 / 1898–1948

Making eye movement recordable

From contact-based devices to photography and early head-mounted equipment, the first challenge was leaving a usable record.

1898–1908Research period / 1908 book
Measurement#01

Leaving a physical trace of eye movement

Huey’s early reading work used contact-based recording equipment, documented in his 1908 monograph. Researchers were turning fleeting eye movements into records that could be inspected.

WHAT CHANGEDA shift from observation toward instrumented recording.
Limitations & reading notes

Contact with the eye affected comfort and constrained natural viewing.

1901Publication year
Measurement#02

Recording the eyes with photography

Dodge and Cline published their study of eye-movement angular velocity using photographic recording, offering an optical route without mechanical attachment to the eye.

WHAT CHANGEDOptical recording became an important non-contact approach.
Limitations & reading notes

Early apparatus still depended on controlled positioning and limited recording and analysis capabilities.

[02] Original paper ↗
1935Publication year
Cognition#03

From reading to looking at pictures

Buswell’s How People Look at Pictures examined fixation distributions and viewing paths over artworks, enabling comparisons of how people explore complex images.

WHAT CHANGEDResearch expanded into picture perception and visual exploration.
Limitations & reading notes

A recorded scanpath does not directly reveal attention, comprehension, or intent.

[03] Original monograph ↗
1948Publication year
Measurement#04

Bringing the recorder with the head

Hartridge and Thomson published methods for investigating eye movements, including an early head-associated recording approach that contributed to later head-mounted tracking.

WHAT CHANGEDResearchers explored alternatives to holding the observer in front of fixed equipment.
Limitations & reading notes

Early head-mounted arrangements still imposed mounting, size, and movement constraints.

[04] Original paper ↗
02 / 1963–1973

Precision and vision science

Magnetic coils and precision optics developed in parallel, while classic experiments connected viewing behavior with the task.

1963Publication year
Measurement#05

Search coils measure rotation through magnetic fields

Robinson introduced scleral search-coil measurement: signals induced in an eye-mounted coil record eye movement in a magnetic field. This is a different branch from camera-based tracking.

WHAT CHANGEDAn important instrument for precise oculomotor research.
Limitations & reading notes

It requires specialized fields and an eye-contact device, rather than a conventional non-contact desktop setup.

[05] Original paper ↗
1965 / 1967Russian / English editions
Cognition#06

The same scene, a different task, a different scanpath

Yarbus’s monograph appeared in Russian in 1965 and English in 1967. His instruction-based experiments made the relationship between task and viewing behavior a landmark research question.

WHAT CHANGEDBeyond where the eyes move: why the observer looks that way.
Limitations & reading notes

The classic examples do not establish reliable mind-reading from an individual scanpath.

1973Publication year
Measurement#07

Dual Purkinje images refine optical tracking

Cornsweet and Crane described a two-dimensional tracker using the first and fourth Purkinje images in infrared, without attaching a device to the eyeball.

WHAT CHANGEDPrecision optical measurement could advance alongside reduced eye contact.
Limitations & reading notes

Optical complexity and working range constrain the setup; not every infrared tracker is a dual-Purkinje system.

[08] Original paper ↗
03 / 1980–1990

From cognition to computer input

Eye movements could test cognitive theories or help people operate computers, creating distinct research and interaction goals.

1980Publication year
Cognition#08

Eye movements test theories of reading

Just and Carpenter’s reading-theory paper connected fixation behavior with comprehension, illustrating the role of eye measurements in cognitive research.

WHAT CHANGEDInstrument output became evidence to test accounts of mental processing.
Limitations & reading notes

Fixation duration requires task and theoretical context; it is not a unique label for a mental process.

[09] Original paper ↗
1989Publication year
Interaction & access#09

Eye gaze becomes computer input

Hutchinson and colleagues described the Erica workstation, estimating screen gaze from eye images to operate menus, including applications as an assistive device.

WHAT CHANGEDEye tracking extended from observation to access and communication.
Limitations & reading notes

Usability depends on selection, accidental activation, calibration, and sustained use—not sampling rate alone.

[10] Original paper ↗
1990Publication year
Interaction & access#10

Distinguishing looking from acting

Jacob’s CHI 1990 paper examined eye-movement interaction techniques, shifting attention from measuring gaze to incorporating it naturally into human–computer dialogue.

WHAT CHANGEDInteraction design became as important as the sensing hardware.
Limitations & reading notes

Looking at an object does not automatically mean activating it; confirmation and feedback still need design.

[11] Original paper ↗
04 / 2001–2016

Easier deployment, open tools, more data

Commercial workflows, open-source platforms, and learned algorithms each lowered different barriers.

2001 / 2002Founding / product milestone
Research platforms#11

Remote eye tracking becomes easier to deploy

Tobii’s company history records its founding in 2001 and first tracker in 2002: a representative commercial milestone in making screen-based research easier to deploy.

WHAT CHANGEDCalibration, acquisition, and analysis increasingly formed product workflows.
Limitations & reading notes

This is a company milestone, not the beginning of all commercial eye tracking.

[12] Manufacturer record ↗
2014Publication year
Research platforms#12

An open wearable platform supports custom research

Kassner, Patera, and Bulling presented Pupil, combining wearable cameras, open-source software, and replay visualization with an emphasis on extensibility.

WHAT CHANGEDResearchers gained more direct control over acquisition and processing.
Limitations & reading notes

Open software does not remove hardware, calibration, or maintenance costs; prototype results do not apply to every later model.

[13] Original paper ↗
2016Publication year
Research platforms#13

Commodity cameras meet learned gaze estimation

GazeCapture / iTracker used phone and tablet data to train a neural network, exploring gaze estimation without additional dedicated sensors. It represents an important software-driven route.

WHAT CHANGEDA broader hardware and participant base became possible.
Limitations & reading notes

Output units, errors, and test conditions differ from dedicated instruments; the approaches are not interchangeable by default.

05 / 2019–2021

Wearables and real-world settings

Natural wear, spatial input, and alternative sensing architectures moved eye tracking beyond the fixed laboratory bench.

2019Manufacturer launch / shipping
Research platforms#14

Learned estimation reaches everyday-looking glasses

Pupil Labs released Pupil Invisible in 2019, bringing learned gaze estimation to an everyday-glasses form factor with a reduced reliance on conventional per-user calibration.

WHAT CHANGEDNatural wear and preparation time became major product goals.
Limitations & reading notes

Calibration-free does not mean error-free; validate quality for the task, lighting, and participants.

Gaze becomes spatial input in see-through AR

HoloLens 2 began shipping in 2019, combining eye tracking, hand interaction, and spatial computing. Permissioned gaze information enabled application interaction.

WHAT CHANGEDGaze extended beyond a 2D screen into spatial directions and targets.

Standard and extended APIs expose different data; permission, calibration, and API support determine availability.

MEMS offers a branch beyond eye-imaging cameras

AdHawk announced MindLink in 2021, using MEMS-based sensing to demonstrate a wearable alternative to conventional video-based tracking.

Different sensing requires checking data definitions and validation; a launch notice does not establish current stock.

XR integrates gaze and avatar expression

Meta Quest Pro became available in 2022 with eye and facial tracking for avatars, social presence, and mixed-reality experiences.

Built-in sensing does not mean all raw signals are exposed to every application.

Official announcements in 2023 document Neon frames and product updates. Its modular design connects sensing hardware, different wearing configurations, and analysis tools.

PARALLEL PATHS, NOT A SINGLE LADDER

New paths do not erase older ones.

A timeline explains change, not a ranking of instruments. Precision measurement, real-world recording, assistive access, and spatial interaction have different goals. The points below are editorial synthesis of the milestones, not benchmark results.

Precision remains a distinct goal

Search coils, precision optics, and high-speed video serve different experimental conditions. A newer release is not automatically a better measurement tool.

Natural activity brings new sources of error

Wearables and learned estimation lower some deployment barriers, but slippage, environments, population differences, and data mapping still require validation.

System interaction and research access differ

Operating a device with your eyes does not establish access to continuous raw data. Research procurement still requires checking APIs, licenses, and synchronization.

METHOD & ORIGINAL SOURCES

Dates with evidence. Interpretation with limits.

Original papers, monographs, and contemporaneous manufacturer records are prioritized, with attributed historical research and official documentation where needed. The chapters and “what changed” statements are editorial synthesis, not a universal academic periodization.

Date rules: ranges denote research periods; 1965 / 1967 distinguishes Yarbus’s Russian and English editions; 2001 / 2002 distinguishes founding and a product milestone; the Neon entry uses 2023 records without inferring first-release dates for every feature. Modern images reuse catalog assets; early line drawings are original concepts, not historical photographs.

Scope: 21 selected milestones, not every researcher, region, vendor, or clinical system. Unverified manufacturer claims of being “the world’s first” are not adopted. Historical launch records do not establish current availability. The catalog’s original product-check dates remain unchanged.
